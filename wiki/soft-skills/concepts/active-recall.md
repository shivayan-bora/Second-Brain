# Active Recall

## Definition

**Active recall** is the practice of producing information **from memory** in a **free, uncued** way — without prompts, without referring to source material. It's the operational form of the *recall* half of [[learning-encoding-and-recall]].

## Why it matters

Most popular study techniques (summarizing, highlighting, re-reading) are **cue-based** — the material is in front of you, prompting your recognition. Recognition feels like understanding; it isn't. The brain only commits to long-term memory what it has to *generate* unprompted. Active recall is the technique that makes generation the unit of study.

## The contrast with summarization

> "Summarization doesn't follow good encoding and retrieval practices which are the corner stone of a good learning technique."

When you summarize a chapter you've just read, the chapter is right there. You're paraphrasing what's already in working memory; nothing has to come from long-term storage. The summary feels productive but creates almost no retention.

Active recall instead asks: **close the book and write what you remember.** The struggle of producing without cues is exactly what consolidates the memory.

## Practical techniques

- **Close-the-tab summaries.** After reading, close the source. Write a summary from scratch. Compare with the original; the gaps are your next study targets.
- **Self-quizzing.** Generate questions while you read; answer them later with no notes.
- **Teach-back.** Explain the concept to someone (or rubber duck, or empty room) without referencing the material.
- **Application transfer.** Apply the idea to a problem you haven't seen before. If you can't, recall isn't yet solid.
- **Spaced retrieval.** Do all of the above tomorrow, in a week, in a month. The intervals expand as the memory firms up.
- **Flashcards (Anki).** The canonical tool for spaced active recall, but be wary — Anki cards that show the *answer* before requiring the *question* are still cued.

## What makes recall "active" specifically

| Cued (passive) | Uncued (active) |
|---|---|
| "Which of these is X?" | "What is X?" |
| Re-reading with highlights | Writing from memory after closing the book |
| Summarizing the chapter you just read | Writing a summary 24 hours later, no notes |
| Glancing at flashcard back too fast | Forcing yourself to produce the answer first |

The distinction is whether your brain has to **generate** the information or whether it's just **recognizing** it.

## When active recall isn't enough

- **No encoding base.** If you've never built the schema (didn't [[priming-and-schema-building|prime]]), recall will just produce "I don't know" repeatedly — that's not desirable difficulty, that's no material to recall.
- **Sequential skills.** Some skills (like motor skills, or accumulating syntax fluency) need lots of doing alongside recall. Active recall isn't a replacement for practice; it's the testing layer.
- **Generative creative work.** "What's a good design pattern for this?" is exploration, not recall. Different mode.

## Examples

- **After watching a video**: pause, look away, write 5 bullets of what you remember. Compare.
- **After reading a chapter**: an hour later, write a teach-back without referring to it.
- **After ingesting a wiki source** (this very project): close the raw and write the summary from memory; compare to the actual notes.
- **In TDD**: writing the test first ([[tdd-red-green-refactor]]) is a form of active recall — you reproduce the API you want before you implement it.
